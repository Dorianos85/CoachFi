## 1. Saving & Emergency Fund

**Why save first.** "Pay yourself first": automate a transfer to savings on payday, before
spending. A fixed automatic transfer beats "saving whatever is left" because it removes willpower
from the loop.

**Emergency fund — the financial seatbelt.**
- Classic target: **3–6 months of essential expenses**, held liquid and separate from daily money.
- **Start small.** Even **one month** of expenses dramatically lowers the chance of falling behind on
  bills. CFPB research: ~40% of people with *no* emergency savings have past-due debt, vs ~5% among
  those with the most savings. The first month matters most.
- Keep it accessible (savings/deposit account), **not invested** — it must be there instantly when needed.

**Deposit safety in Poland (BFG).** Bank deposits are guaranteed by the **Bankowy Fundusz Gwarancyjny**
up to the **PLN equivalent of €100,000 per depositor, per bank** (combined across that person's accounts),
paid out in złoty. Higher temporary protection applies to specific sums (e.g. property sale, inheritance,
severance). Covers domestic banks and SKOK credit unions.

**Saving tactics that work.**
- 30-day rule: wait 30 days (or 24–72h for smaller items) before non-essential purchases.
- Sinking funds: save a little monthly for known future costs (car service, holidays, taxes) so they
  never become a shock or a loan.
- Consistency > size: 10% saved every month beats occasional large, irregular amounts.

## 2. Household Budgeting

**Track before you budget.** Record real spending for ~**30 days** first — most households find
10–15% "leakage" on non-essentials they didn't notice.

**50/30/20 rule** (a simple starting framework):
- **50% Needs** — rent/mortgage, utilities, food, transport, minimum debt payments.
- **30% Wants** — eating out, entertainment, subscriptions.
- **20% Savings & extra debt repayment.**
- Adapt in high cost-of-living situations (e.g. 60/20/20). It's a guideline, not a law.

**Zero-based budgeting.** Give *every* unit of income a job until **income − all allocations = 0**
(savings counts as a "job"). Nothing is left unassigned to leak away.

**Needs vs wants.** The discipline is in honestly classifying borderline items. Rent and basic
groceries are needs; the upgraded phone and the fourth streaming service are wants.

**Cash-flow timing.** Budgeting is also about *when* money arrives vs leaves. Aligning bills and
transfers with payday prevents avoidable overdrafts.

## 3. Investing (long-term, beginner-safe)

**Foundations first.** Build an emergency fund and get **high-interest debt under control before**
investing in volatile assets — otherwise a market dip can force you to sell at the worst time.

**Core principles (SEC / Investor.gov / FINRA):**
- **Diversification** — "don't put all your eggs in one basket." Spread money across many investments
  so one loser doesn't sink you. A broad index fund can hold *thousands* of companies in one product.
- **Asset allocation** — how you split between **stocks / bonds / cash** drives most long-run risk and
  return. Match it to your goal and time horizon.
- **Rebalancing** — periodically restore your target mix (trim what grew, top up what shrank) so risk
  doesn't drift.
- **Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** — invest equal amounts at regular intervals regardless of price.
  You automatically buy more when prices are low, less when high, and avoid trying to time the market.
- **Time in the market > timing the market** — staying invested consistently beats jumping in and out;
  missing just a handful of the best days over decades can halve returns.

**Index funds & ETFs.**
- Index fund tracks a market index (e.g. S&P 500, MSCI World, WIG20). An **ETF** is similar but trades on
  an exchange like a stock.
- Advantages: low ongoing fees (TER), broad diversification, simplicity.
- **Fees compound against you.** Over 30 years, ~1%/year vs ~0.2%/year can cost a large share of the final
  pot. Low cost is one of the few things an investor can control.

**Risk vs return.** Higher expected return generally means higher risk (more ups and downs). Be deeply
sceptical of anything promising "high returns, no risk."

**Speculative assets (e.g. crypto).** Highly volatile — treat as a *small* slice (rule of thumb: ≤5%) and
only money you could afford to lose entirely. Never borrow to buy them.

## 4. Compound Interest & Core Math

**Compound interest** — interest earns interest. Formula `A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n·t)`. Time matters more than
amount: starting 10 years earlier can roughly double the final outcome.

**Rule of 72** — divide 72 by the annual return to estimate years to double. At 8% → 72 ÷ 8 ≈ **9 years**.

**The two-edged sword.** The same compounding that grows investments grows *debt*. Underestimating how fast
unpaid interest accumulates is a leading cause of **debt spirals**.

**Real vs nominal return.** Real return ≈ nominal return − inflation. A 5% return with 5% inflation
preserves purchasing power but doesn't grow it.

## 5. Debt & Credit

**Good vs bad debt.** A mortgage at a low rate can be reasonable; **credit-card debt (often ~18–24% APR) is
almost always bad** — pay it in full monthly and never carry a balance you can avoid.

**Two payoff strategies (CFPB framing) — neither is universally "better":**
- **Avalanche (highest interest first)** — mathematically cheapest; saves the most money overall.
- **Snowball (smallest balance first)** — slower on cost but delivers quick wins that keep you motivated.
- Choose by what keeps *you* going. Always keep minimum payments on everything and an emergency fund.

**Pay-off vs invest.** If debt interest > ~6–7%, clear debt first. Below that, splitting can make sense.
Keep the emergency fund either way.

**Credit history in Poland (BIK).** Score factors mirror global ones: payment history, utilisation,
length of history, credit mix, new inquiries. Improve it by paying on time and keeping utilisation low
(< ~30%). Check your BIK report periodically.

## 6. Macroeconomics (what moves the backdrop)

**Inflation.** Sustained rise in the general price level; money loses purchasing power. At ~5%/year, money
loses roughly **40% of its value over 10 years** — the reason not to hold large idle cash.

**Inflation targeting — NBP (Poland).** Since 2004 the National Bank of Poland targets **2.5% inflation with
a symmetric ±1 percentage point band**, measured by the annual CPI change. It's a **medium-term** goal, so
inflation can temporarily run above/below.

**Why ~2–2.5%, not 0%?** A small positive buffer supports growth and keeps a safety margin away from
**deflation**. (For comparison, the US Federal Reserve and ECB target ~2%.)

**Monetary policy = interest rates.** The central bank's main tool is the **benchmark interest rate**.
- **Raise rates →** borrowing gets more expensive → households/firms spend less → demand and inflation cool.
- **Cut rates →** borrowing gets cheaper → spending and investment rise.
- This is the "interest-rate channel" and it directly hits **variable-rate loans** (your payments rise when
  rates rise).

**Deflation risk.** Persistently falling prices make people delay purchases, shrink demand, and make existing
debts heavier in real terms — a self-reinforcing downward spiral. That's why mild positive inflation is the goal.

**GDP & recession.** **GDP** = total value of goods and services an economy produces — the broadest activity
gauge. A **recession** is a significant, broad decline in activity (commonly flagged by two consecutive
quarters of falling GDP).

## 7. Retirement in Poland (the three pillars)

Poland's system: **ZUS** (state pension) + workplace **PPK** + individual **IKE / IKZE**.

**PPK (Pracownicze Plany Kapitałowe)** _(2026)_ — workplace auto-enrolment:
- **Employee** basic contribution **2%** of salary (can be reduced toward 0.5% for lower earners, keeping
  employer and state additions); optional extra up to 2%.
- **Employer** adds a basic **1.5%** (optional extra up to 2.5%) — effectively *free money*.
- **State** adds a one-off **250 PLN welcome payment** + **240 PLN annual bonus**.

**IKE vs IKZE** — individual tax-advantaged accounts, two different "when" for the tax break:
- **IKE** — no upfront deduction, but **gains are tax-free** on a qualifying withdrawal (after the age/holding
  conditions). Annual limit **28,260 PLN** _(2026)_.
- **IKZE** — **deduct contributions now** (reduce taxable income), then pay a **flat 10%** on withdrawal.
  Annual limit **11,304 PLN** _(2026)_; self-employed **16,956 PLN** _(2026)_.

**Rules of thumb.** Aim to save ~15% of gross income for retirement from your mid-20s; a common target nest egg
is ~25× annual expenses (linked to a ~4% withdrawal rate). Start early — compounding does the heavy lifting.

## 7A. International Markets — Country Reference

Coach FI ships in 9 locales, so Mila should adapt country-specific facts to the user's market.
The *principles* (emergency fund, diversification, compound interest) are universal; the *vehicles*,
*deposit guarantees* and *central-bank targets* differ. All figures _(2026)_ — re-verify yearly.

### Deposit guarantees (how much of your bank savings is protected)

| Market | Scheme | Coverage per depositor, per bank |
|---|---|---|
| 🇵🇱 Poland | BFG | PLN equivalent of **€100,000** |
| 🇪🇺 Eurozone/EU (ES, FR, DE, …) | National DGS (EU-harmonised) | **€100,000** (or local-currency equivalent) |
| 🇺🇸 USA | FDIC | **$250,000** (per ownership category) |
| 🇬🇧 UK | FSCS | **£120,000** (raised from £85,000 on 1 Dec 2025) |
| 🇯🇵 Japan | DICJ | **¥10,000,000** + interest (yen deposits) |
| 🇮🇳 India | DICGC | **₹5,00,000** (₹5 lakh) |
| 🇧🇷 Brazil | FGC | **R$250,000** per institution (R$1M cap / 4 years) |
| 🇹🇷 Turkey | TMSF | **₺1,200,000** (adjusted annually) |
| 🇨🇳 China | Deposit Insurance | **¥500,000 RMB** per account |

> Note: deposit guarantees protect *bank deposits*, **not** investments, pension funds or insurance
> products. In Brazil, government bonds (**Tesouro Direto**) aren't FGC-covered but carry the sovereign
> guarantee instead.

### Central-bank inflation targets

| Central bank | Target |
|---|---|
| Fed (US), ECB (euro area), Bank of England, Bank of Japan | **2%** |
| NBP (Poland) | **2.5%** (±1 pp) |
| RBI (India) | **4%** (±2 pp, retained 2026–2031) |

### Tax-advantaged retirement & savings accounts by market _(2026)_

- **🇺🇸 USA** — **401(k)** workplace plan: employee limit **$24,500** (50+ up to ~$32,500). **IRA / Roth IRA**:
  **$7,500** ($8,600 if 50+). Roth = tax-free growth; Traditional = deduct now.
- **🇬🇧 UK** — **ISA**: ~**£20,000/year** tax-free wrapper (cash or stocks & shares). **Workplace pension**
  auto-enrolment (earnings trigger ~£10,000) with employer contributions.
- **🇫🇷 France** — **Livret A** (tax-free regulated savings), **PEA** (equities, tax-advantaged after 5 years),
  **assurance-vie** (flexible long-term wrapper), **PER** (retirement plan, contributions deductible).
- **🇪🇸 Spain** — **Plan de pensiones**: deductible up to **€1,500/year** individual, up to **€10,000** with
  an employer plan (≈€8,500 employer + €1,500 employee).
- **🇯🇵 Japan** — **New NISA**: tax-free, **¥3.6M/year** (¥2.4M growth + ¥1.2M tsumitate), **¥18M** lifetime cap.
  **iDeCo**: contributions tax-deductible, locked until age 60.
- **🇮🇳 India** — **PPF** (~7.1%, 15-yr, EEE, Section 80C), **NPS** (mixed equity/bond pension), **ELSS**
  (equity funds, 3-yr lock, 80C).
- **🇧🇷 Brazil** — **Tesouro Direto** (government bonds, sovereign guarantee), **previdência privada**
  **PGBL** (deduct now, taxed on withdrawal) / **VGBL** (no deduction, tax only on gains).
- **🇹🇷 Turkey** — **BES** (Bireysel Emeklilik Sistemi): personal pension with a **~30% state contribution** on top.
- **🇨🇳 China** — bank deposits insured to ¥500k; long-term savers use the state pension plus voluntary
  private pension accounts.

**Cross-market rule of thumb Mila can always say:** "Wherever you live, the order is the same —
emergency fund first, then a low-cost diversified retirement account that gets a tax break or an employer/state
match, before anything speculative."

## 8. Net Worth & Financial Health

- **Net worth = assets − liabilities.** Track the *trend* monthly, not the absolute number.
- **Financial resilience** = ability to absorb shocks (job loss, big bill) and recover. It's about
  preparedness, not wealth level — an emergency fund is its backbone.
- Financial well-being has present *and* future components: control over day-to-day finances, capacity to
  absorb a shock, being on track for goals, and freedom to make choices.

---

## 9. Family & Protection

- **Life insurance** = income replacement for dependents if the primary earner dies. If people rely on your
  income, you likely need it. It is not an investment product.
- **Couples**: both partners should understand the full picture and plan together — transparency reduces
  conflict and improves decisions.
- **Teaching kids**: pocket money *with rules* (e.g. save/spend/give split) teaches budgeting through real,
  low-stakes decisions.
